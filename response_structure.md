# Section 4. Contribution 1: Stochastic and Empirical Modeling of Human-Driver Behavior in Mixed AV-Human Traffic

## Technical Subtitle

Generalized stochastic car-following framework, controlled HV-following-AV field experiment, mixed-traffic behavioral dataset, and AV appearance / behavioral adaptation finding.

---

## 4.A. Problem Before My Work

Explain in plain English:

Autonomous vehicles will share roads with human drivers for many years. Researchers, engineers, and government agencies therefore need realistic ways to model how human drivers behave in mixed AV-human traffic.

Before my work, the field faced two related gaps:

1. Many car-following models treated human driving behavior as deterministic or did not adequately capture uncertainty, variation, and heterogeneity in human behavior.
2. Researchers lacked controlled real-world evidence showing whether human drivers behave differently when following an autonomous vehicle rather than a human-driven vehicle.

Define key terms for the officer:

- **Mixed traffic:** traffic where autonomous vehicles and human-driven vehicles operate together.
- **Car-following model:** a model that predicts how a vehicle follows the vehicle ahead.
- **Stochastic:** accounting for uncertainty and variation.
- **Controlled field experiment:** a real-world experiment designed to observe a specific behavior under controlled conditions, rather than passively collecting ordinary traffic data.

---

## 4.B. What I Created and Why It Was Original

Explain narrowly:

I contributed a more realistic modeling foundation for mixed AV-human traffic by combining:

1. A generalized stochastic car-following framework that accounts for uncertainty and variation in human driving behavior; and
2. Controlled empirical evidence showing how human drivers behave when following an autonomous vehicle, including the finding that AV recognizability and subjective trust affect driver behavior.

The originality is not that I worked on "traffic simulation" generally. The originality is that I addressed a specific missing link in mixed-traffic modeling: how to represent uncertain human-driver behavior and how to incorporate observed behavioral adaptation to AVs.

## Required Distinction

## Technical Subtitle

---

## 5.A. Problem Before My Work

Explain in plain English:

Autonomous and connected vehicles must operate around human-driven vehicles that may cut in, change lanes, slow down unpredictably, or disrupt traffic flow. Earlier AV/CAV planning and control methods often relied on simplified assumptions, fixed input structures, or idealized traffic behavior. These assumptions limited their usefulness for real-world mixed traffic, where AVs must make safe, efficient, smooth, and reliable decisions around human drivers.

Define key terms:

- **Trajectory optimization:** choosing a vehicle's speed and path to improve safety, travel time, fuel use, or comfort.
- **Reinforcement learning:** an AI method where a system learns decisions by testing actions and receiving rewards or penalties.
- **Proactive longitudinal control:** controlling vehicle motion in advance to manage or prevent disruptions caused by human-driven vehicles.
- **Benchmark:** a reference method or dataset that others use to test whether their own systems work well.
- **Deployment constraint:** a real-world limitation such as missing data, variable input size, safety requirements, computational limits, or unpredictable human behavior.

# Appendix A. Writing Rules for the AI Agent

## Use First Person

Use "I," "my," and "me."

Do not write in third person unless quoting or summarizing third-party letters.

## Do Not Overclaim

Avoid saying:

- "My work was adopted by the government" unless there is objective documentary evidence.
- "My patents prove major significance" unless there is proof of use.
- "My publications alone show originality."
- "My citation counts alone show major significance."

Prefer:

- "Independent researchers used my method as a benchmark."
- "This report cited and applied my framework to..."
- "This project relied on my data to calibrate..."
- "The organizer invited me because..."
- "The patent evidence corroborates practical translation."

## Always Tie Evidence to One of the Two Contributions

For every exhibit, explain:

1. What evidence is shown.
2. Which contribution it supports.
3. What part of the contribution was used or relied upon.
4. Why that use shows major significance.
5. How it helps answer the officer's targeted concern.

## Separate Objective Evidence From Expert Explanation

Preferred order:

1. Define original contribution.
2. Explain my personal role.
3. Present objective evidence of use/adoption/reliance.
4. Explain why that use matters.
5. Use expert letters to interpret the objective evidence.

## Citation Context Matters More Than Citation Count

For every citation-based exhibit, explain whether the later work:

- Adopted my method.
- Used my data.
- Extended my experiment.
- Used my work as a benchmark.
- Relied on my findings.
- Built a new system based on my framework.
- Applied my work in government, industry, or policy context.

## Cross-Sector Evidence Is Supportive, Not Standalone

Panel discussions, training, NSF support, and advisory roles should be used as:

- Evidence of reliance on my technical expertise.
- Evidence of practical translation.
- Evidence of sustained recognition.
- Evidence supporting final merits.

They should **not** be framed as a separate third original contribution.
