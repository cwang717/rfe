\section{Contribution 1: Human-Driver Behavior Around Autonomous Vehicles in Mixed Traffic}

\textbf{Technical scope:} Generalized stochastic car-following framework, controlled human-driven-vehicle-following-autonomous-vehicle (HV-following-AV) field experiment, mixed-traffic behavioral dataset, and AV appearance / behavioral adaptation finding.

\subsection{Problem Before My Work}

Autonomous vehicles will not replace human-driven vehicles all at once. For many years, public roads will operate in mixed traffic, where autonomous vehicles and human-driven vehicles share the same roadway, respond to one another, and jointly affect safety, efficiency, and public acceptance.

For autonomous vehicles to be deployed safely in this environment, the field must answer a practical question: \textbf{how do human drivers behave around autonomous vehicles, and how can that behavior be modeled and predicted?}

Before my work, the field lacked an adequate foundation for answering that question.

First, many existing traffic models treated human drivers as if they reacted according to fixed and predictable rules. In reality, human drivers vary widely. Some are cautious, some are aggressive, some hesitate, and the same driver may behave differently under different conditions. These small and uncertain reactions can propagate backward through traffic and create stop-and-go waves. The field therefore needed a way to model uncertainty in human car-following behavior, rather than assuming that human drivers always follow deterministic rules.

Second, the field lacked controlled real-world evidence on how human drivers behave when following an autonomous vehicle. Researchers could model how a human-driven vehicle follows another human-driven vehicle, and how an autonomous vehicle follows another vehicle. But there was limited evidence on the specific mixed-traffic case in which a human-driven vehicle follows an autonomous vehicle. Without such evidence, many models had to assume that a human driver would treat an autonomous vehicle the same way as any other lead vehicle. That assumption was unproven.

Third, mixed traffic in real urban environments requires more than a simple one-leader, one-follower model. In congested urban traffic, a vehicle's future movement may be affected by multiple surrounding vehicles, intersections, pedestrians, bicycles, buses, and frequent stop-and-go conditions. Autonomous vehicles therefore need prediction methods that can identify which surrounding vehicles matter most and anticipate human-driven vehicle movements under complex interaction.

Fourth, mixed-traffic data are limited and difficult to generalize. Data collected in one city, country, or driving environment may not directly transfer to another because driving habits, traffic rules, road design, and data availability can differ. This creates a practical deployment problem: even if a prediction model works in one environment, it may fail in another unless it can adapt efficiently when new-environment data are limited.

These were not isolated technical inconveniences. Together, they limited the field's ability to evaluate mixed-traffic safety, calibrate realistic AV deployment simulations, predict surrounding human-driven vehicles, and transfer prediction models across deployment environments. The field therefore needed an integrated foundation connecting human-driver uncertainty, real-world human-following-AV evidence, interaction-aware prediction, and limited-data adaptation.

\subsection{What I Created and Why It Was Original}

My contribution is a framework for understanding, measuring, and predicting how human drivers behave around autonomous vehicles in mixed traffic.

This contribution is not merely ``traffic simulation'' or ``traffic prediction'' in general. The specific contribution is narrower: \textbf{I helped the field model and predict human-driver behavior in the presence of autonomous vehicles, especially when that behavior is uncertain, affected by trust in AVs, and difficult to generalize from limited data.}

These components directly addressed the four gaps identified above.

First, in my first-authored stochastic car-following work, I created a generalized stochastic framework for representing uncertain human following behavior. Instead of treating human drivers as if they always follow fixed deterministic rules, my work modeled human car-following behavior as variable and uncertain. This allowed researchers to analyze how small stochastic reactions by individual drivers can propagate through a traffic stream and affect traffic stability. This was original because it provided a more realistic analytical foundation for studying human-driven traffic behavior, especially in mixed traffic where human drivers interact with autonomous vehicles.

Second, through the controlled human-driven-vehicle-following-autonomous-vehicle field experiment, I helped provide real-world evidence for a specific mixed-traffic condition that had not been adequately measured: how human drivers behave when following an autonomous vehicle. My work showed that human-driver response depends not only on the movement of the lead vehicle, but also on whether the lead vehicle is recognizable as an autonomous vehicle and on the driver's subjective trust in AV technology. This was original because it challenged the assumption that human drivers would necessarily treat AVs the same way as ordinary human-driven vehicles.

Third, I contributed to interaction-aware trajectory-prediction methods for complex traffic environments. In real traffic, an autonomous vehicle must anticipate not only the vehicle directly ahead, but also nearby vehicles whose movements may affect the target vehicle's future path. My work helped develop prediction approaches that identify important surrounding vehicles and account for heterogeneous vehicle interactions in congested urban traffic. These prediction methods are part of Contribution 1 because they address the same deployment problem from the AV's perspective: an autonomous vehicle cannot safely plan in mixed traffic unless it can predict the uncertain future movements of surrounding human-driven vehicles.

Fourth, I developed domain-adaptive prediction and transfer-learning methods for limited-data driving environments. A prediction model trained in one environment may not work well in another environment because traffic patterns, driver behavior, and available data can differ. My work addressed this practical limitation by developing methods for transferring prediction knowledge across driving environments when data in the new environment are limited. This was original because it moved mixed-traffic prediction research toward deployment conditions, where large, perfectly matched datasets are often unavailable.

The originality of my contribution lies in connecting these components into one practical foundation for mixed traffic. I did not merely study traffic behavior in a general sense. I addressed a specific missing link in autonomous-vehicle deployment: \textbf{how to model, measure, predict, and generalize human driving behavior around autonomous vehicles.}

Prior work often treated human driving behavior as deterministic, lacked controlled field evidence for human-following-AV behavior, focused on simpler traffic settings, or assumed that prediction models trained in one environment could be reused without addressing domain differences. My work helped move the field toward a more realistic approach: human drivers are uncertain; their behavior around AVs can change when the AV is recognizable; complex traffic prediction must account for interactions among multiple vehicles; and prediction models must adapt when mixed-traffic data are scarce or domain-specific.

The publications, datasets, models, and patents discussed below are therefore not presented as publications alone. They are evidence of the underlying contribution: a set of original methods and empirical findings that helped the field better understand and predict human-driver behavior around AVs. The following evidence shows that later researchers and public-sector transportation professionals used these components not merely as published literature, but as experimental evidence, modeling assumptions, datasets, benchmarks, and technical foundations for subsequent mixed-traffic and AV-deployment research.

\section{Contribution 2: Autonomous-Vehicle Planning and Control for Real-World Mixed Traffic}

\textbf{Technical scope:} Trajectory optimization, learning-based motion planning, and proactive vehicle control methods that help connected and autonomous vehicles operate safely and efficiently around human drivers at road segments, intersections, and roundabouts.

\subsection{Problem Before My Work}

Autonomous and connected vehicles must operate in mixed traffic, where human-driven vehicles and automated vehicles interact with each other. This is not only a vehicle-control problem. It is also a human--machine interaction problem: the automated vehicle's trajectory can affect nearby human drivers, and human drivers' responses can in turn affect safety, efficiency, comfort, fuel or energy use, and traffic stability.

While Contribution~1 addressed how human drivers behave around autonomous vehicles, Contribution~2 addresses the corresponding planning and control problem: \textbf{how autonomous and connected vehicles should plan and control their own trajectories when human-driver responses, roadway-specific constraints, and traffic-system-level outcomes must be considered together.}

Before my work, the field lacked a deployable mixed-traffic planning and control framework that could connect three issues that were often studied separately: vehicle-level trajectory optimization, human-driver interaction, and system-level traffic performance.

First, many trajectory-planning and control methods focused primarily on the automated vehicle's own motion. Classical trajectory optimization research studied how to improve safety, travel time, fuel or energy use, and comfort. Learning-based autonomous-driving research studied how vehicles can make data-driven motion decisions. Traffic-flow research studied congestion, oscillation, and system-level traffic impacts. However, these research streams were often separated. As a result, the field lacked a clear framework for evaluating an AV/CAV trajectory not only by whether the automated vehicle completed its own maneuver, but also by how that maneuver affected surrounding human-driven vehicles and the broader traffic stream.

Second, many existing methods were developed for simplified road segments or idealized traffic settings and could not be directly applied to complex roadway structures. At signalized intersections, vehicles must coordinate with traffic signals, stopping behavior, acceleration and deceleration patterns, and infrastructure guidance. At roundabouts, vehicles must negotiate merging, yielding, circulating traffic, and geometric constraints. In these environments, a generic vehicle-control method is often insufficient because safety and efficiency depend on roadway-specific interaction patterns. The field therefore needed structure-aware planning and control methods for roadway environments where human--AV interaction, infrastructure, and traffic geometry jointly determine performance.

Third, earlier AV/CAV planning and control methods often treated human-driven vehicles as external disturbances that the automated vehicle should avoid or react to. But in mixed traffic, a connected or automated vehicle can do more than react. Through its own longitudinal behavior---such as adjusting speed, creating gaps, smoothing traffic flow, or assisting a lane change---a CAV can influence human-driver behavior and reduce traffic disruption before it fully develops. The field therefore needed proactive control methods that could manage or prevent disruptive human-driver behavior, rather than merely respond after a cut-in, lane change, or stop-and-go disturbance had already occurred.

In plain English, the problem was this: real mixed traffic is an interaction system. Automated vehicles do not move in isolation. Their planning and control decisions affect human drivers, and human drivers' responses affect the safety, smoothness, efficiency, energy performance, and stability of the traffic stream. My work addressed how to design optimization- and learning-based trajectory planning/control methods for this human--AV interaction environment under real-world deployment constraints.

In this response, I use ``planning and control'' to refer to methods that determine vehicle trajectories, speed profiles, longitudinal actions, or cooperative maneuvers under safety, efficiency, comfort, energy, computation, infrastructure, and human-interaction constraints.

This contribution addressed the practical control problem that follows from Contribution~1. If human drivers behave uncertainly around AVs, respond differently to automated vehicles, and create disturbances such as lane changes, braking waves, and merging conflicts, then autonomous and connected vehicles cannot be planned as isolated ego vehicles. I helped create a connected body of methods and frameworks that moved AV/CAV trajectory planning and control from idealized vehicle motion toward deployable mixed-traffic interaction. The originality of this contribution lies in three connected components.

First, I helped establish a mixed-traffic planning and control framework that evaluates CAV trajectories not only by the automated vehicle's own feasibility, but also by their effects on surrounding human-driven vehicles and the broader traffic stream. This framework connected trajectory optimization, learning-based motion planning, and traffic-flow-aware evaluation. Third, I helped develop proactive longitudinal-control methods that use CAV behavior to manage or prevent disruptive human-driver behavior. Through its own longitudinal motion, the CAV can adjust speed, create safer gaps, smooth traffic flow, assist a human-driven vehicle's lane change when appropriate, or reduce the likelihood that a disruptive lane change will damage traffic stability. This reframed the CAV from a passive follower or ego-vehicle optimizer into an active participant in a human--machine traffic system. By selecting its own longitudinal behavior, the CAV can influence nearby human-driver behavior and improve the overall traffic outcome.

These components were connected by a common technical idea: \textbf{AV/CAV planning and control should be designed for mixed-traffic interaction, not only for isolated automated-vehicle motion.} My contribution helped the field evaluate and design CAV trajectories based on how they affect human drivers, roadway-specific interactions, safety, traffic smoothness, efficiency, and fuel or energy performance.

The originality of this contribution was therefore not that I worked on autonomous-vehicle planning and control generally. The originality was that I addressed the real-world mixed-traffic version of the problem: how automated and connected vehicles can plan and control their trajectories when human drivers respond to them, when roadway structures impose special interaction constraints, and when CAVs can proactively influence human-driver behavior to improve system-level outcomes.
