Dear Officer:

I am pleased to provide this letter regarding Dr. Yu Wang and her invited participation in the **2025 Southeast Wisconsin Transportation Symposium**, specifically the panel titled **“Workshop Panel Discussion Questions – Advancing AV/CV Readiness in Wisconsin.”** I served as an organizer for this symposium and was involved in inviting panel participants with relevant expertise in automated vehicles, connected vehicles, transportation technology, and public-sector readiness.

The 2025 Southeast Wisconsin Transportation Symposium was organized to support knowledge exchange among public transportation officials, academic researchers, and industry professionals. Although the symposium title itself does not describe the event as a government training program, a major practical purpose of the event was to help public-sector transportation professionals better understand emerging transportation technologies and their implications for Wisconsin. **Based on the registration records and attendee list for the symposium, approximately [__%] of the participants were affiliated with the Wisconsin Department of Transportation or related public transportation agencies.** The audience therefore consisted substantially of government transportation personnel who were responsible for understanding, planning for, or responding to changes in transportation technology.

One of the reasons this symposium was important is that state transportation agencies are often required to make planning, policy, infrastructure, and safety decisions involving technologies that are developing quickly in both industry and academia. Automated vehicles and connected vehicles are examples of such technologies. Government agencies need access to current technical knowledge, but they may not always have direct exposure to the most recent research, field deployment experience, or industry-facing methods. The symposium was designed to help bridge that gap by bringing together people from government, academia, and industry to discuss what these technologies mean in practice.

The panel **“Advancing AV/CV Readiness in Wisconsin”** was intended to help Wisconsin transportation stakeholders understand the technical, operational, safety, and policy questions associated with automated and connected vehicle readiness. The discussion focused on issues such as how agencies should evaluate emerging vehicle technologies, what kinds of evidence are useful for understanding their real-world impacts, and how public agencies can prepare for deployment in a realistic and responsible way. The panel was not merely an academic presentation session. It was structured as a practical discussion for an audience that included public officials and transportation professionals seeking to understand current approaches and unresolved challenges in the field.

I invited Dr. Wang to participate in this panel because of her recognized technical contributions to the study of **human-driver behavior in mixed autonomous-vehicle and human-driven traffic**. My decision to invite her was not based simply on the fact that she had published papers. Rather, I considered the substance of her work and her ability to explain technically complex mixed-traffic issues in a way that would be useful to a government and practitioner audience.

In particular, Dr. Wang’s work is valuable because it addresses a practical question that public agencies must understand when preparing for AV/CV deployment: automated and connected vehicles will share roads with human-driven vehicles for many years, and agencies need to understand how human drivers behave around these vehicles. Dr. Wang’s research contributes to this issue by helping model, measure, and predict human-driver behavior in mixed AV-human traffic.

One important aspect of Dr. Wang’s work is her contribution to **stochastic modeling of human car-following behavior**. Rather than assuming that all human drivers respond in a fixed or uniform way, her work recognizes that human-driver responses are variable and uncertain. This type of modeling is important for public-sector transportation planning because traffic safety, flow stability, congestion, and operational performance all depend on how human drivers actually respond to vehicles around them, including automated vehicles.

Another important aspect of Dr. Wang’s work is her use of **controlled field-experiment data** to study how human drivers behave when following an autonomous vehicle. This is especially relevant to public agencies because AV/CV readiness cannot be based only on theoretical assumptions or technology demonstrations. Agencies need empirical evidence about real driver behavior, including whether human drivers respond differently when a vehicle is recognizable as an autonomous vehicle and whether trust in AV technology affects driving behavior. Dr. Wang’s work directly addresses these questions through field-data-based analysis of mixed traffic.

For these reasons, I believed Dr. Wang would add meaningful value to the panel. The panel’s purpose was to help Wisconsin transportation stakeholders think realistically about AV/CV readiness. Dr. Wang’s expertise was relevant because her work addresses one of the technical foundations of that readiness question: how human drivers behave around automated vehicles, how that behavior can be modeled and predicted, and what types of evidence should inform public-sector evaluation of AV/CV deployment.

Dr. Wang’s participation therefore reflected independent recognition of her expertise by the symposium organizers. We invited her because her work had relevance beyond ordinary academic publication. Her research addressed practical problems that transportation agencies and practitioners need to understand, and her perspective helped support the symposium’s goal of improving public-sector knowledge of automated and connected vehicle readiness.

In my opinion, being invited to participate in this panel was a meaningful professional recognition. Panelists were selected because they had specialized expertise that could benefit a government and practitioner audience. Dr. Wang’s invitation reflected my view that her contributions in transportation engineering, especially in the areas of **human-driver behavior modeling in mixed AV-human traffic, field-data-based analysis of human-following-AV behavior, and empirical evaluation of human adaptation to automated vehicles**, were important and relevant to the field.
