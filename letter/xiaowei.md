Re: Support Letter for Dr. Yu Wang

Dear Officer:
I am pleased to provide this letter regarding Dr. Yu Wang and her invited participation in the 2025 Southeast Wisconsin Transportation Symposium, specifically the panel entitled “Workshop Panel Discussion Questions – Advancing AV/CV Readiness in Wisconsin.” I served as host for this symposium and was responsible for identifying and inviting panel participants with expertise in automated vehicles, connected vehicles, transportation technology, and public-sector transportation readiness.
The 2025 Southeast Wisconsin Transportation Symposium was co-hosted by the UWM Institute for Physical Infrastructure and Transportation and the Wisconsin Department of Transportation. State transportation agencies play a central role in making planning, policy, infrastructure, and safety decisions related to rapidly evolving transportation technologies emerging from both industry and academia. Automated vehicles (AVs) and connected vehicles (CVs) are prominent examples of such technologies. Effective decision-making in this area requires access to current technical knowledge, although public agencies do not always have direct exposure to the latest research findings, deployment experiences, or industry practices. The symposium was designed to help bridge this gap by facilitating dialogue among government agencies, academic researchers, and industry professionals regarding the practical implications of these emerging technologies. Approximately 50% of participants were affiliated with the Wisconsin Department of Transportation or related public transportation agencies. 
The panel “Advancing AV/CV Readiness in Wisconsin” was intended to provide transportation stakeholders with an evidence-based perspective on the technical, operational, safety, and policy considerations associated with automated and connected vehicle deployment. The discussion addressed issues such as how transportation agencies should evaluate emerging vehicle technologies, what forms of evidence are most useful for assessing their real-world impacts, and how public agencies can prepare for deployment in a realistic and responsible manner. Rather than serving solely as an academic presentation session, the panel was structured as a practical discussion for an audience that included public officials and transportation professionals seeking insight into current approaches, opportunities, and unresolved challenges in the field.
I invited Dr. Wang to participate in this panel because of her recognized technical contributions to the study of human-driver behavior in mixed autonomous-vehicle and human-driven traffic environments. My decision to invite her was based not merely on her publication record, but on the substantive contributions of her research and its relevance to issues currently facing transportation agencies and practitioners. Equally important was her ability to communicate technically complex concepts in a manner that would be informative and accessible to a government and practitioner audience.
In particular, Dr. Wang’s work addresses a fundamental issue associated with AV/CV deployment that is of direct relevance to public agencies: understanding how human drivers interact with automated vehicles in mixed-traffic environments. As automated and connected vehicles are expected to share roadways with human-driven vehicles for many years, transportation agencies must understand the behavioral dynamics that arise in these interactions. Dr. Wang’s research contributes to this need by helping model, measure, and predict human-driver behavior in mixed AV-human traffic.
A notable contribution of Dr. Wang’s research is her work on stochastic modeling of human car-following behavior. Rather than assuming uniform driver responses, her research recognizes and quantifies the inherent variability and uncertainty in human driving behavior. This line of work is particularly relevant to transportation planning and operations because traffic safety, flow stability, congestion, and overall system performance are strongly influenced by how human drivers respond to surrounding vehicles, including automated vehicles. By providing more realistic representations of driver behavior, such research can improve the accuracy of transportation analyses and readiness assessments.
Equally significant is Dr. Wang’s use of controlled field-experiment data to investigate driver responses when following autonomous vehicles. This work has direct implications for public agencies, as AV/CV readiness assessments must be informed by empirical evidence rather than relying solely on theoretical assumptions or technology demonstrations. Transportation agencies need a clear understanding of how human drivers behave in real-world settings, including whether driver behavior changes when a vehicle is recognizable as autonomous and how perceptions of and trust in AV technology influence driving decisions. Dr. Wang’s work directly addresses these questions through rigorous field-data-based analysis of mixed traffic environments.
Given the practical relevance of her research and her expertise in the Autonomous Driving industry, the symposium organization committee considered Dr. Wang well qualified to contribute to this discussion. During the panel, she offered valuable insights regarding challenges associated with deploying automated vehicle technologies in real-world transportation systems. In particular, she discussed how uncertainty in human-driver responses and interactions between human-driven and automated vehicles can complicate deployment efforts and affect public acceptance, operational performance, and safety outcomes. She also contributed to discussions concerning how government agencies and academic institutions can better support deployment initiatives. Drawing upon her research and her practical experience, Dr. Wang emphasized the importance of empirical evidence, realistic evaluation methodologies, and a deeper understanding of human behavioral adaptation when assessing AV technologies and informing transportation policy and planning decisions. Her contributions provided transportation stakeholders with a practical perspective on issues directly relevant to AV/CV readiness and deployment.
In my professional judgment, Dr. Wang’s invitation to serve as a panelist constituted a meaningful form of professional recognition. Panel participants were selected on the basis of specialized expertise and their ability to contribute valuable perspectives to an audience consisting of transportation professionals, researchers, and public-sector decision makers. Dr. Wang’s selection reflected the significance and practical relevance of her contributions in transportation engineering, particularly her work on human-driver behavior in mixed AV-human traffic environments, field-data-driven analysis of interactions between human drivers and autonomous vehicles, and empirical evaluation of behavioral adaptation to emerging vehicle technologies.
