Dear Officer:

I am pleased to provide this letter regarding Dr. Yu Wang and her invited participation in the **Autonomous Mobility panel at the 5th Annual E-Mobility Summit**. I served as the Director for Strategy and Partnerships for the Georgia Network for Electric Mobility (GNEM) and was responsible for identifying and inviting panelists with the expertise needed to address a multidisciplinary audience on the future of autonomous and electric mobility.

The 5th Annual E-Mobility Summit, held in Georgia, brought together stakeholders from government agencies, industry, academia, and the nonprofit sector to discuss the intersection of electrification, automation, infrastructure, and policy. The Autonomous Mobility panel, titled **"Autonomous Mobility: The New EV Frontier,"** was designed to explore how artificial intelligence, simulation environments, intelligent infrastructure systems, and safety validation are enabling real-world autonomous operations on campuses, in cities, and along controlled corridors. The panel's goal was to give attendees a clearer understanding of how autonomy is being operationalized today and what it takes to prepare regions for what is next.

I personally invited Dr. Wang to serve as a panelist. My decision was based on the substance of her recognized technical expertise, her professional background as a Lead Autonomy Engineer in the autonomous-vehicle industry, and her ability to speak to the practical deployment challenges that the panel was convened to address. Dr. Wang was, to the best of my knowledge, **the only invited panelist whose primary professional domain was autonomous vehicles**, and she was uniquely positioned to cover the topic from both academic research and industry deployment perspectives.

The panel discussion was organized around five thematic blocks of questions, and Dr. Wang made substantive contributions across several of them. Three aspects of her input are particularly worth highlighting.

**First, Dr. Wang addressed how her organization moves from simulated environments to real-world testing and ultimately to scaled deployment.** The panel opened with questions about the pathway from simulation to real-world operations: how organizations currently transition from simulated testing to on-road deployment, how deployment and expansion opportunities are vetted internally, and how the alignment between intelligent infrastructure, V2X systems, and vehicle intelligence is evolving. Dr. Wang was the panelist best positioned to speak to these questions from direct industry experience. She described how her organization uses simulation-based validation and AI-driven methods to test autonomous-vehicle behavior before deploying on public roads, and she explained the internal decision process for selecting deployment sites—what technical, operational, and regulatory factors are weighed, and how simulation results, real-world data, and infrastructure conditions inform those decisions. Drawing on her deep technical background in vehicle trajectory optimization and learning-based motion planning, she also discussed how vehicle intelligence and infrastructure systems can complement each other: for instance, how variable speed guidance at signalized intersections can work alongside on-vehicle planning to smooth traffic flow, and why coordination between infrastructure-based sensing and vehicle-based decision-making becomes especially important in complex roadway environments. For an audience that included government officials, infrastructure planners, and economic development professionals, this was valuable because it grounded high-level discussions of "simulation," "AI," and "V2X" in the concrete engineering and operational realities that determine whether autonomous vehicles can deploy safely and scale practically.

**Second, Dr. Wang addressed what makes certain cities and roadway environments viable for autonomous vehicle deployment.** The panel was asked what cities are successfully hosting autonomous systems today and what makes those environments viable for early adoption; what an ideal real-world testing environment looks like; and how city governments and state DOTs are adapting roadway standards and traffic systems to support autonomous vehicles. Dr. Wang contributed an important perspective grounded in her organization's real-world deployment experience. She explained that not all roadway environments present the same operational challenge—controlled corridors, signalized intersections, roundabouts, and dense urban streets each impose different interaction patterns, geometric constraints, and infrastructure requirements. Drawing on her research and industry practice, she discussed why autonomous-vehicle planning and control methods cannot be treated as one-size-fits-all across different roadway structures, and why understanding the specific demands of each environment is essential for selecting deployment sites and scaling operations. She also spoke to the practical pain points that arise when moving from pilot to scaled deployment—where the breakdowns tend to occur, and why technology, regulation, and partnerships must all be addressed together rather than in isolation. Her perspective helped attendees from state and local transportation agencies understand that preparing a region for autonomous mobility involves more than building charging infrastructure or adding lane markings. It requires thinking about how vehicle behavior interacts with roadway geometry, traffic signal systems, and the broader traffic stream.

**Third, and distinct from the specific technical and operational topics above, Dr. Wang brought a cross-sector perspective that no other panelist could offer.** The panel was deliberately structured to include voices from government, industry, academia, and economic development, because autonomous mobility is not a challenge that any single sector can address alone. Among the invited panelists, Dr. Wang was the only participant whose primary professional domain was autonomous vehicles, and—critically—she was uniquely positioned to bridge two worlds that are often disconnected: academic research and commercial deployment. She could speak credibly about peer-reviewed research in transportation engineering while also drawing on firsthand experience as a Lead Autonomy Engineer developing autonomous-driving systems under real-world operational, safety, and business constraints. During the discussion, she addressed the question of where things tend to break down when moving from pilot to deployment—technology, regulation, partnerships, or something else—and argued that the most persistent challenges are not purely technical. Progress, she explained, requires sustained collaboration across academia, industry, and government: academic research provides foundational understanding of problems such as human-driver behavior and vehicle control; industry translates that understanding into working systems; and government agencies create the regulatory, safety, and infrastructure framework within which deployment occurs. She also emphasized that the most practically impactful research directions often emerge not from the academic literature alone, but from the technical problems that engineers encounter when building and testing autonomous systems—a perspective that resonated strongly with an audience trying to understand what it actually takes to operationalize autonomous mobility. This cross-sector lens, combined with her standing as the only autonomous-vehicle domain expert on the panel, was a significant reason I selected her, and her contribution was valued by the other panelists and the audience alike.

I would also note that Dr. Wang's invitation was not based on general professional reputation alone. I reviewed her background and selected her because her specific technical expertise matched the substantive needs of the panel. The panel required someone who could address the real-world foundations of autonomous-vehicle deployment—how organizations move from simulation to deployment, how they choose where to operate, and how vehicle technology, infrastructure, and regulation must evolve together—in a way that was accessible to a multidisciplinary audience. Dr. Wang's combination of academic research depth and industry deployment experience made her the right person for that role.

In my opinion, being invited to participate as a panelist at the E-Mobility Summit reflects meaningful recognition of Dr. Wang's expertise. Panelists were selected because they could contribute specialized knowledge to a discussion that mattered to stakeholders across government, industry, and academia. Dr. Wang's invitation reflected my assessment that her work at the intersection of autonomous-vehicle research and real-world deployment was important, original, and relevant to the future of transportation.
