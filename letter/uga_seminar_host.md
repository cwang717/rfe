To Whom It May Concern:
I am Qianwen ("Cami") Li, Ph.D., Assistant Professor in the School of Environmental, Civil, Agricultural and Mechanical Engineering at the University of Georgia. Based on my awareness of her work and its relevance to ongoing developments in future mobility, I invited Dr. Wang to deliver a seminar in our Future Mobility Living Lab Seminar Series on February 20, 2026, in Athens, Georgia. I also organized a roundtable discussion with our graduate students to provide additional opportunities for technical exchange. I am pleased to provide this letter regarding Dr. Wang's invited seminar and the professional qualifications that led me to that invitation.
My decision to invite Dr. Wang was based on the substance of her technical expertise and her unique professional background. I was particularly familiar with her research on human-driver behavior and mixed-traffic interactions involving autonomous vehicles, an area that I consider fundamental to the successful deployment of automated transportation systems. The challenge of enabling autonomous vehicles to operate safely and efficiently alongside human-driven vehicles remains one of the most important unresolved issues in the field. Dr. Wang's work addresses this challenge through the development of behavioral models, empirical studies, and data-driven analyses that improve our understanding of how human drivers respond to and interact with autonomous systems. Combined with her current role as a Lead Autonomy Engineer at May Mobility, this expertise made her exceptionally well qualified to provide our students with perspectives that bridge both research and real-world implementation.
The Future Mobility Living Lab Seminar Series is designed to expose graduate students to current research directions that have practical importance for the future of transportation. Our students are training to enter a field where autonomous vehicles and human-driven vehicles will share the same roads for many years, and they need to understand not only the academic foundations but also the real-world challenges that industry practitioners face. Dr. Wang's seminar directly addressed this need.

Dr. Wang organized her talk around several challenges involved in translating autonomous-vehicle research into real-world deployment. A central example throughout the seminar was the problem of operating autonomous vehicles in mixed traffic environments, where autonomous and human-driven vehicles must interact safely and efficiently. She explained that successful deployment requires more than advances in vehicle technology alone; it also requires a deep understanding of human driving behavior and the uncertainty associated with human decision-making. Drawing on her research and development experience, Dr. Wang discussed how variations in driver behavior—including differences in reaction time, following behavior, and responses to autonomous vehicles—can influence traffic performance and system safety. She explained how researchers use behavioral modeling, simulation, and empirical data to better understand these interactions and to evaluate potential deployment strategies. Particularly valuable was her discussion of how insights generated through academic research can inform the development and validation of autonomous-driving systems in industry. This topic resonated strongly with our graduate students because it demonstrated how fundamental transportation-engineering research can be applied to address one of the most significant practical challenges facing autonomous-vehicle deployment today.

The second theme addressed how data and real-world evidence can inform autonomous-vehicle development. Dr. Wang drew on her experience with both controlled field experiments and large-scale vehicle data to discuss what kinds of evidence are needed to validate AV behavior in mixed traffic. She explained why simulation alone is not sufficient, why controlled experiments that capture actual human responses to autonomous vehicles provide insights that purely theoretical models cannot, and how data collected in one driving environment may or may not transfer to another. For our graduate students, many of whom are learning research methods in transportation engineering, this discussion was valuable because it showed how rigorous empirical work connects to practical deployment questions.

What made Dr. Wang's seminar particularly valuable, however, was her third theme: lessons from working at the intersection of academia and industry in autonomous driving. Drawing on her experience both as a researcher who has published in leading transportation journals and as an engineer developing autonomous-driving systems in industry, Dr. Wang offered candid reflections on how AV development actually happens. She discussed why progress in this field requires collaboration across academia, industry, and government---academic research provides foundational understanding, industry translates that understanding into working systems under real-world constraints, and government agencies provide the regulatory and safety framework within which deployment occurs. She emphasized that the most impactful research questions often emerge not from the academic literature alone, but from the practical problems that engineers encounter when building and testing autonomous systems. She also shared her perspective on what kinds of skills and research mindsets are most valued in the autonomous-driving industry today. This type of insight is something our students rarely receive from speakers whose experience is limited to university research, and it was a significant reason I invited Dr. Wang.

Following the seminar, Dr. Wang participated in a roundtable discussion with our graduate students. This informal session focused on career development in industry and applied research settings. Students asked about the transition from academic research to industry practice, how to identify research directions that have both intellectual value and practical relevance, and what day-to-day engineering work in autonomous driving actually involves. The session was well attended, and several students told me afterward that Dr. Wang's ability to speak credibly about both research and real-world deployment helped them think more concretely about their own career paths.

Dr. Wang’s invitation to speak in our seminar series reflected our assessment that her expertise would be of substantial value to our academic community. In selecting seminar speakers, I seek individuals who can offer perspectives that extend beyond a traditional academic presentation and expose students and faculty to issues shaping the future of the field. Dr. Wang was particularly well positioned to do so because of her combination of research expertise in human-driver behavior and mixed-traffic systems and her firsthand experience developing autonomous-driving technologies in industry.
Her presentation provided a perspective that is rarely available within a single speaker, connecting fundamental research questions with the practical realities of autonomous-vehicle development and deployment. This contribution filled an important gap in our students’ educational experience and generated significant interest and engagement among both students and faculty members. The positive response to her seminar further reinforced my view that she brought meaningful and distinctive expertise to our program.
