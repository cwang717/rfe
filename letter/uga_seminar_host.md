Re: Support Letter for Dr. Yu Wang

Dear Officer:

I am pleased to provide this letter regarding Dr. Yu Wang and her invited seminar at the University of Georgia. I hosted Dr. Wang as a speaker in our **Future Mobility Living Lab Seminar Series** on February 20, [Year], in Athens, Georgia. I personally extended the invitation and organized both the seminar and an associated roundtable discussion with our graduate students.

My decision to invite Dr. Wang was based on the substance of her technical expertise and her unique professional background. Dr. Wang has conducted significant research on **human-driver behavior and mixed-traffic modeling in the context of autonomous vehicles**, and she currently works as a Lead Autonomy Engineer at May Mobility, an autonomous-vehicle company. I sought a speaker who could bring both research depth and real-world industry experience to our graduate students, and Dr. Wang's background made her an especially strong candidate.

The Future Mobility Living Lab Seminar Series is designed to expose graduate students to current research directions that have practical importance for the future of transportation. Our students are training to enter a field where autonomous vehicles and human-driven vehicles will share the same roads for many years, and they need to understand not only the academic foundations but also the real-world challenges that industry practitioners face. Dr. Wang's seminar directly addressed this need.

Dr. Wang organized her talk around three main themes. The first theme addressed **how to model and understand human driver behavior in mixed traffic with autonomous vehicles**. She explained that for autonomous vehicles to be safely deployed, the field must account for the fact that human drivers do not behave in fixed or perfectly predictable ways. She discussed how uncertainty in driver behavior---such as variations in following distance, reaction time, and trust in autonomous technology---can affect overall traffic flow, and why understanding this human element is essential for both academic research and practical AV development. This topic resonated with our graduate students because it connected fundamental transportation-engineering questions to a concrete deployment challenge that the autonomous-driving industry is actively working on.

The second theme addressed **how data and real-world evidence can inform autonomous-vehicle development**. Dr. Wang drew on her experience with both controlled field experiments and large-scale vehicle data to discuss what kinds of evidence are needed to validate AV behavior in mixed traffic. She explained why simulation alone is not sufficient, why controlled experiments that capture actual human responses to autonomous vehicles provide insights that purely theoretical models cannot, and how data collected in one driving environment may or may not transfer to another. For our graduate students, many of whom are learning research methods in transportation engineering, this discussion was valuable because it showed how rigorous empirical work connects to practical deployment questions.

What made Dr. Wang's seminar particularly valuable, however, was her third theme: **lessons from working at the intersection of academia and industry in autonomous driving**. Drawing on her experience both as a researcher who has published in leading transportation journals and as an engineer developing autonomous-driving systems in industry, Dr. Wang offered candid reflections on how AV development actually happens. She discussed why progress in this field requires collaboration across academia, industry, and government---academic research provides foundational understanding, industry translates that understanding into working systems under real-world constraints, and government agencies provide the regulatory and safety framework within which deployment occurs. She emphasized that the most impactful research questions often emerge not from the academic literature alone, but from the practical problems that engineers encounter when building and testing autonomous systems. She also shared her perspective on what kinds of skills and research mindsets are most valued in the autonomous-driving industry today. This type of insight is something our students rarely receive from speakers whose experience is limited to university research, and it was a significant reason I invited Dr. Wang.

Following the seminar, Dr. Wang participated in a **small roundtable discussion with our graduate students**. This informal session focused on career development in industry and applied research settings. Students asked about the transition from academic research to industry practice, how to identify research directions that have both intellectual value and practical relevance, and what day-to-day engineering work in autonomous driving actually involves. The session was well attended, and several students told me afterward that Dr. Wang's ability to speak credibly about both research and real-world deployment helped them think more concretely about their own career paths.

In my opinion, Dr. Wang's invitation to speak in our seminar series reflects recognition of her expertise by an academic institution separate from her own affiliated universities and employer. I invited her because she could offer something that few speakers can: substantive knowledge of human-driver behavior and mixed-traffic research, combined with firsthand experience developing autonomous-driving systems in industry and a thoughtful perspective on how these two worlds connect. Her visit filled an important gap in our students' training, and the response from both students and faculty was strongly positive.
